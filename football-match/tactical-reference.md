# Tactical reference (football-* skills)

Established coaching/analytics frameworks, distilled for pre-match analysis. Read fully once when writing tactical/game-state judgements; cite what the sheet shows, never invent what it does not. Sources: Coaches' Voice, Spielverlagerung, StatsBomb, published substitution/game-state research (links in `~/football-corpus.local/ANALYTICS-NOTES.md` reading list).

## 1. Game phases and game flow
A match cycles through four phases per team; who wins the transitions usually wins the match:
1. **Build-up** (GK + defenders organising out from the back) — slow, low risk, draws the press.
2. **Progression** (through midfield) — where pressure is applied or absorbed.
3. **Final-third attack** — few touches, high value; xG per shot rises sharply inside the box.
4. **Defensive phases**: high press (own final third of opponent), mid-block (~own half), low block (own third). Blocks concede territory, not necessarily chances — a compact low block shrinks xG per shot against more than raw shot counts suggest.

**Transitions** (ball just won or lost) produce a disproportionate share of goals: the defence is un-set, rest defence (players left behind the ball: 2 CBs + 1-2 pivots) is decisive. A side whose full-backs and wingers both push high leaves the rest defence exposed to one long pass — this is the single most common structural route to conceding.

**Game-state flow** (measured): the side scoring first wins ~69%; ~63% of goals come in the 2nd half as games open up. Leading teams cede possession and shot volume but keep xG per shot low against; trailing teams rack up shots late that are low-quality — a late shot barrage is not the same as late pressure. A match projected tight at half-time is not yet an under.

## 2. Pressing triggers (when a coordinated press jumps)
Standard triggers the sheet's habits can corroborate: back-pass to the keeper; receiver facing his own goal or receiving on the half-turn under pressure; pass to the touchline (sideline as extra defender); a poor/slow first touch; a slow or one-footed receiver; goal kicks played short. If the sheet shows a side that builds short AND the opponent presses high, the turnover-in-own-third route is live: name who errs in build-up and which defenders are exposed. A press that jumps on triggers tires after ~60 minutes — late fades are real.

## 3. Shapes and what they mean
- **Back four** (4-3-3/4-2-3-1): balanced; the 4-2-3-1 double pivot protects transitions, the single pivot frees an 8 but demands rest-discipline from full-backs.
- **Back three / 3-5-2**: 3+2 build-up beats a two-man press; wing-backs provide width but a back three with both wing-backs high leaves 3 v 3 or worse on the counter; a back five (3-5-2 without the ball dropping to 5-4-1) is a drawish/low-xG signal.
- **Mid-block 4-4-2/4-1-4-1**: cedes possession, counters into channels behind advancing full-backs.
- Asymmetry matters more than the nominal formation: one side's overload (a 3v2 on a flank, an extra man between the lines) is where chances come from. Judge "who is weak on paper" by zone, not by formation name.

## 4. In-game adaptation and the bench
- **Substitution timing**: research and practice converge on the attacking sub at 55-70 minutes (trailing side), not the last 10 minutes where few touches decide anything. A bench with a genuine game-changer changes the late-game goal expectation more than a tired starter.
- **Manager adaptation markers**: dropping the block 10-15 metres after scoring; switching to a back five to protect a lead; moving the press onto a struggling full-back in the 2nd half; half-time shape changes (recorded at HT in half-time counts). A manager with a documented in-game fix (strong 2nd-half goal difference after HT deficits) is a real, citable edge.
- **Substitution habits**: a side that changes early (before 60') reacts; a side whose subs come at 75'+ rides its starters — fatigue risk in congestion, and a thin bench for comebacks.

## 5. Matchup logic (how to connect the pieces)
1. **Press v build-up** (already in the sheet): press strength v build-up bravery = turnover route.
2. **Flank v flank**: a winger/full-back pair that attacks a zone against a defence that concedes chances from that zone (the sheet's flank and compactness numbers).
3. **Set pieces**: ~1 in 5 goals from set plays including penalties (measured, our corpus); check both sides' set-piece xG for and against and aerial strength before any other chaos factor.
4. **Tempo and context**: heat, congestion, a side content with a point lowers tempo and total goals; a must-win opens the game late regardless of shapes.
5. **Style clashes resolve predictably**: possession side v compact block = wasted-chances risk for the favourite (the deep block's low xG-against shows it); counter side v high line = transition goals.

## 6. What the data cannot see (name as unknowns, not guesses)
Fresh tactical installs (a new manager's system needs 5-10 matches), new signings integrating, dressing-room state, referee strictness shifting the card/foul game, wind/pitch. These are uncertainty notes in the answer, never silent adjustments.
